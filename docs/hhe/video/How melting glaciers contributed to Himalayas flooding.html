GEOFF BENNETT: What makes that part of the Himalayas so vulnerable?

DAN SHUGAR: That’s a good question.

I suppose it would be a couple of things, one, incredibly steep topography and then huge relief, so massive differences between the low points and the high points, lots of glaciers, lots of glacial lakes, which weren’t an issue in this particular disaster, but certainly in other ones.

A lot of seismic activity.

The Gorkha earthquake wasn’t that long ago, and we saw very similar sort of disasters unfold, in fact, just on the other side of this mountain.

And the human part of that equation, I think, is important.

Unlike in Western North America, in much of the Himalaya, you have settlements in these incredibly high, narrow valleys that essentially are putting people and infrastructure and livelihoods at risk when these landslides or these floods come down.

GEOFF BENNETT: The other human element at play, it strikes me, is climate change.

DAN SHUGAR: We can speak in generalities, because it’s a little bit hard to speak about the specific event just yet because we haven’t done any sort of attribution study.

As temperatures rise, glaciers will melt, will thin vertically, destabilizing the valley walls that they formerly were sort of help – hoping – or helping to hold up.

Additionally, as air temperatures rise, the permafrost is changing, and so permafrost is just ground that remains frozen year-round year after year.

But in some areas, both at high altitudes, but also in the north, permafrost is destabilizing because of warming temperatures.

And that can cause large landslides like this as well.

And then a third factor, I think would be changes in precipitation patterns, not only in terms of intensity, but in terms of when during the year they occurred.

Water can get underneath the glaciers, allowing them to slide a little bit more rapidly, any number of ways that that extra water can sort of enhance or increase the likelihood of one of these kinds of events.

GEOFF BENNETT: Dan, what lessons can be learned from this disaster?

DAN SHUGAR: I think that these landscapes, these steep mountain landscapes, are continually evolving due to climate change.

GEOFF BENNETT: Dan Shugar with the waterSHED Lab at the University of Calgary, thanks for joining us this evening.
