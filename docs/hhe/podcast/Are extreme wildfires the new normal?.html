ZEKE HAUSFATHER: There’s a lot of different regional trends that are underneath that.

HAUSFATHER: The typical fire burns about three times more area than it did 30 years ago or 40 years ago, which is largely driven by changes in conditions. The vegetation is drier, and so when a spark catches, it’s more likely to burn a larger area.

ROTT: Yeah, climate change– don’t know if you’ve heard of it, small thing–

BARBER: Yeah.

ROTT: –happening globally.

ROTT: Yeah, well, unlike a generic cartoon villain, you know, it’s not easily vanquished by Captain Planet and his gang of trusted friends.

ROTT: Zeke says that rising temperatures nurture fires that might otherwise just die out. So, like, take Canada, for example.

HAUSFATHER: So, every one degree centigrade, or roughly two degrees Fahrenheit, of warming during the fire season in Canada– so May to September– is associated with an 80% increase in the amount of area burned that year.

BARBER: 80%– that’s a huge jump for just, like, a couple of degrees Fahrenheit.

ROTT: Yeah, it’s massive. And he says, since the 1960s, the fire season has warmed up about four degrees Fahrenheit on average. So–

BARBER: Wow.

ROTT: –that translates to a huge amount of burned area in Canada because when it’s warmer, things just dry out quicker, so there’s literally more fuel for the fire.

BARBER: So doesn’t this also have to do with humans not doing, like, prescribed burns to clear the underbrush?

ROTT: In the United States, yes, that’s definitely true. So, in many parts of the country, not just the Western US, forests are adapted to fire. In many cases, they need it ecologically. And, you know, Native communities used fires for centuries to forests, but since at least the 1930s, we’ve been suppressing forest fires, putting them out as quickly as we can.

BARBER: I mean, Nate, only you can prevent forest fires.

ROTT: Yes, God bless Smokey Bear. It’s not Smokey the Bear, Gina. It’s Smokey Bear. Don’t let the Forest Service come after you.

BARBER: I had no idea, honestly.

ROTT: Yeah. And look, like, Smokey’s slogan was great to protect people and homes and, you know, maybe some timber, but it’s not so great to keep fuel levels down in those forests.

HAUSFATHER: When fires do occur in those forests, there’s so much undergrowth that’s accumulated that the fires are much more catastrophic. They reach up into the canopy of the trees. They become what we call crown fires that actually kill the trees–

BARBER: Geez.

HAUSFATHER: –rather than what historically has happened in Western forests, where you have these low-level fires that kill the undergrowth, but don’t reach up and actually kill the trees. The boreal forest is a different beast.

ROTT: Yeah, and the boreal forests in Canada, the ones that are smoking up parts of the US right now, those have never needed prescribed burning in the same way.

HAUSFATHER: The boreal forest–

BARBER: Wow.

HAUSFATHER: –in Canada is 4 and 1/2 times the size of Texas. And so, historically–

BARBER: What?

HAUSFATHER: –when fires burn in those forests, they’re just allowed to burn themselves out. You know, they haven’t had the same history of fire suppression that we have in the US that has built up fuel.

ROTT: So what he’s kind of saying is, it is normal for these big fires to happen in Canada, but what’s not normal is how often these huge fires are happening.

BARBER: So, historically, how often is normal for them to burn?

ROTT: So, typically, in Canada’s western boreal forest, Zeke says big fires would happen, you know, once every 50 to 200 years.

BARBER: That is not happening right now.

ROTT: No, it is much more frequent. And–

BARBER: Yeah.

ROTT: –when we’re talking about boreal forests, you know, for you non-ecology nerds out there, you know, we’re talking about these dense forests that stretch across the Northern Hemisphere, and temperatures are rising at higher latitudes faster than they are closer to the equator.

HAUSFATHER: What we’re seeing now is considerably more frequent fires, because these areas that used to be much wetter– you know, much of the the northern boreal resembles a swamp– are now much drier because of higher temperatures.

ROTT: And it’s not necessarily that more fires are starting, you know, which, in both the US and Canada, is often caused by lightning, sometimes caused by people. It’s that a warmer climate is creating the conditions for fires to get going and burn a lot more vegetation when they do.

BARBER: OK, so I grew up in the Pacific Northwest, and there weren’t a lot of wildfires. And then I had my own kid, and then wildfires and smoke were just part of the summers. And honestly, I thought I was going to just, like, avoid all that and move to the East Coast.

ROTT: Yeah.

BARBER: And once I’m here, I’m not going to have any of that smoke.

ROTT: Yeah.

BARBER: But for listeners in the same boat as me– like, now, I’m inhaling this smoke– like, what are the recommendations for staying healthy?

ROTT: Well, so the biggest piece of advice I could give you, Gina, is to monitor the air quality to figure out your exposure to risk. So, in the US–

BARBER: OK.

ROTT: –there’s an air quality index for how polluted–

BARBER: Yeah.

ROTT: –the air is in a certain area. You know, it’s based on how many pollutants are in the air, and that includes particulate matter, these tiny little particles that are so small, they can get into your bloodstream.

BARBER: Oh, my gosh.

ROTT: You know, there’s been a lot of research that shows that it’s particularly risky for pregnant people. So I’d highly recommend people use AQI as kind of a quick pulse check to figure out how good the air quality is. It goes from green, where the air pollution isn’t a risk to people, all the way up to maroon, which means it’s a risk for everybody. And there is limited guidance for, you know, when you should limit outdoor activity, for example. But Lisa Patel, a pediatrician and environmental scientist at Stanford Medicine Children’s Health, says it may not be a good enough tool for wildfires.

LISA PATEL: These air quality guidelines were built off the burning of fossil fuels. There was a study that was done on children between zero to five that found that wildfire smoke is about 10 times as toxic. 10 times as many children showed up to the emergency room with respiratory complaints.

ROTT: And it’s not just kids, right? Like, inhaling that smoke can lead to inflammation, which is linked to cancer, to heart and lung disease, and to dementia.

BARBER: Oof. So, what else can we do to limit our exposure to wildfire smoke?

ROTT: Yeah, so spending more time inside, you know, though, I should say, you know, staying inside won’t totally keep you from being exposed, especially in older homes with a lot of drafts. But, you know, staying inside is one piece of advice– wearing a mask when you go outside, recirculating the air in your car, you know, so you’re not sucking air directly from outside into the car. And using an air filter indoors goes a long way. Lisa says you can make one with a HEPA filter duct taped to a box fan. I’ve actually been to places where they’re doing this in Missoula, where I grew up. They are creating them for people that can’t afford air filters in their house. And when it comes to this bigger, you know, staying inside part that we’re talking about, Lisa knows that advice might be tough to hear.

PATEL: I’m a parent myself. My kids were 2 and 5 when– and we were in the middle of a pandemic. What we are in is a situation where– and it is sad for me to say. I grieve about it when I have to say it, but we’re increasingly having periods of time where it’s not safe for our kids to be outdoors.

ROTT: But she also says this is part of our new normal, which is what I also heard from Zeke.

HAUSFATHER: Like, we’re not going to be able to put the fire genie back in the bottle and return to the climate we had in the 1980s, at least not anytime in the next few centuries. So, it’s not a good news story.

BARBER: Geez.

HAUSFATHER: But I also don’t want to let people off the hook, you know. We need to adapt–
